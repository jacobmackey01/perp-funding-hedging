Perpetual futures: funding, carry and hedging

This study uses Binance perpetual futures on BTC, ETH and SOL to test three things. Does the funding paid by leveraged longs give a carry trade that is worth its risk? Does shorting perps make a spot portfolio safer, and at what cost? Does funding or open interest warn of crashes? Rules were tuned on data to 2022-12 and then judged once on 2023-01 to 2026-10.

Findings

  1. The funding premium has mostly gone. Shorting BTC perps paid 31% a year in 2021 and 38% for ETH. Since then it has fallen to 5.1% and 4.9% in 2025, and 2.9% and 1.9% annualised so far in 2026. That is at or below the 3-month T-bill yield (3.7% in 2026).
  2. Out of sample, cash-and-carry barely beat T-bills. With rules picked on 2019–2022 data, BTC+ETH carry returned 5.1% a year from 2023, against 4.6% for T-bills. The picked rules only entered when funding was high, so they spent most of the time in T-bills (13% of days in the trade for BTC, 45% for ETH). Staying in the trade all the time at 1x would have returned 3.5% (BTC) and 3.5% (ETH), below T-bills.
  3. The real risk in carry is leverage on the short leg, and it is very uneven across assets. With no top-up, a 3x short is liquidated by a 33% rise in the mark price. BTC's largest 7-day rise was 43%; ETH's was 77% and SOL's 120%. Even with the margin topped up weekly, an always-on 3x carry was liquidated 6 times on ETH in training and 11 times on SOL out of sample; on BTC it was liquidated 2 times in total. Funding can also flip hard: in the FTX week, SOL shorts paid funding at 1490% a year.
  4. Trend-based perp hedges cut drawdowns, at a cost in return. Out of sample, the trend rule picked on training data (hedge 100% when price < 200-day average) fell 32% at its worst, against 60% unhedged, and returned 28% a year against 38%. A permanent 50% hedge fell 35% and returned 26%. Because longs usually pay shorts, a hedge earns funding while it is on; the funding received partly offsets what the hedge gives up.
  5. Funding signalled turbulence, not direction. For BTC and ETH, the highest-funding fifth of days was followed by a 10%+ fall within a week more often than other days. Out of sample that was 22% vs 9% for BTC and 50% vs 18% for ETH (the ETH figure rests on only 32 days). Average returns after those days were not lower, though, because crowded markets also kept rallying. A regression of next-week returns on funding, OI change, past return and basis had a negative out-of-sample R² for all three assets (-0.031 to -0.007), so it forecast worse than the historical average. Hedging only when funding was crowded (hedge 50% when 7-day funding > 50% a year) did no better than staying unhedged.
  6. Crashes show up in the funding data during the fall, not reliably before it. In the 6 worst BTC weeks since 2019, BTC funding was lower during the fall than in the week before in 6 of 6 cases. Funding was above 30% a year going into 2 of them (Feb 2021, May 2021) and at ordinary levels before the rest.

2. Cash-and-carry: long spot, short perp

The trade holds spot and shorts the same number of coins in the perp. It earns funding whenever longs pay shorts, and idle cash earns the T-bill rate. Leverage applies to the short leg only, so at Lx the trade needs 1 + 1/L dollars of capital per dollar of position. Why the mark price matters: biggest last-trade wicks against a short

Last-trade highs on the perp include flash wicks that the mark price never reached. On 13 March 2020 the ETH perp printed a last-trade high 203% above the previous close, while its mark price rose 31%. A backtest using last-trade highs would show liquidations that the exchange's own rules would not have triggered.

Method

  • Data. Binance USDT-margined perpetuals and spot markets: daily candles, perp mark-price candles, and every funding payment (BTC from September 2019, ETH from November 2019, SOL from September 2020). Daily open interest and a second funding series come from Bybit. The cash benchmark is the 3-month US T-bill yield (FRED DTB3). Prices are in USDT, treated as USD.
  • SQL layer. SQLite does the daily funding aggregation, the spot/perp/mark panel, the trailing signals (7- and 30-day funding, OI change, volatility, moving averages), the forward outcomes, the yearly regimes, the venue cross-check and the market tracker. A test checks the SQL signals against pandas.
  • Carry engine. Each day it marks the short to market against posted margin and credits funding on the previous close's notional. It checks for liquidation at the day's mark-price high, with 0.5% maintenance margin, and resizes both legs to target leverage on the reset schedule. Entry and exit use the previous day's 7-day funding, so decisions are lagged by one day.
  • Hedge engine. Hedge targets are decided at the previous close. The short earns or pays funding and its mark-to-market, and it is re-sized when it drifts more than 10% from target. The hedge is assumed fully collateralised by the coins held.
  • Out-of-sample. Training runs to 2022-12-31 and testing from 2023-01-01. Rules and signal cut-offs were fixed on training data. Costs are 7.5 bps per leg per unit traded, tested from 0 to 30 bps.
  • Not modelled. Exchange counterparty risk (FTX-style failures), USDT de-pegging, withdrawal limits, borrow and transfer frictions, tiered maintenance margin for large positions, and regulatory limits on venue access (for example, which venues a UAE-regulated firm may use). Results are for one venue and one decade.

This is historical research, not investment advice. Leverage, perpetual futures and exchange custody can lose more than the capital at stake.
